A little context

The mind,
not the résumé.

I am difficult to describe by listing what I do, because the list makes it sound sequential. It isn’t.

I build software, reverse-engineer systems, help people find their way into technical fields, write fiction, read philosophy, and play Magic. Somewhere underneath all of that, there is probably music playing.

I work at the seam between business processes and software engineering: translating between people who know what they need and machines that require considerably more precision.

I don’t experience engineering, philosophy, fiction, technology, faith, and curiosity as sealed compartments. An API teaches me something about boundaries. A poem gives me a better model of choice. The subjects keep exchanging information.

I like systems, but I care about the humans inside them.

Patterns of thought

How I think.

01

Parallel

I keep several models alive at once. Subjects do not wait politely in separate queues, and they frequently exchange information.

02

Structural

I want the architecture underneath the thing: the incentives, assumptions, dependencies, failure modes, and hidden constraints.

03

Translational

I move between abstraction and implementation. An engineering problem may reveal a human one; a philosophical question may suggest a better design.

04

Attentive

I notice wording, exceptions, and the sentence that does not quite fit. The anomaly is often where useful information is hiding.

05

Practical

What can we try? What is the next step? A good model should help someone do something with the problem in front of them.

06

Deliberate

When the words matter, I like to write them down. Writing gives me time to examine what I mean and leave room for a thoughtful response.

The human part

What matters
to me.

I am a Christian, an empiricist, and a rationalist in the broad sense. I want my convictions to survive contact with questions.

Human agency matters to me in everyday life, too. I want people to have the information and space to make their own choices, including choices I would not make.

Useful can be very ordinary: explaining an unfamiliar process, working through the next step, or checking back to see whether the thing actually got easier. Ideally, someone leaves with more room to act for themselves.

Precision, patience, and basic kindness belong in the same conversation. I want to hold a conviction, hear a difficult answer, and take responsibility when I get something wrong.

A guide to talking with me

Conversation protocol.

  1. Bring the real question.

    The one underneath the polite version is usually more interesting.

  2. Disagree cleanly.

    Let’s get precise enough to locate the actual disagreement.

  3. Give the answer room.

    Some questions deserve a pause or a longer written reply.

  4. Do not simplify yourself.

    Technical, strange, unfinished, contradictory, niche—I would rather have the high-resolution version.

  5. Ask for help.

    Computers, software, manufacturing, technical careers, or figuring out what question you should be asking.

  6. Rabbit holes are permitted.

    There is a non-zero chance your original question will eventually become philosophy.
